Neuroscience · Unit 3

Glial
Cells

Nervous tissue is neurons plus glial cells. This unit covers all major glial types — their locations in the CNS and PNS, functions, and clinical relevance.

Astrocytes

CNS only
  • The most functionally diverse glial cells in the nervous system
  • Found exclusively in the brain and spinal cord
  • Have multiple processes ("foot processes") radiating from the cell body, allowing them to contact both neurons and blood vessels simultaneously

Function 1 — Blood-brain barrier (BBB)

BBB — 3-layer structure

① Endothelial cells — inner lining of blood vessel; joined by dense tight junctions that restrict permeability (this is the key barrier layer)
② Basal lamina — connective tissue / protein layer just outside the endothelium
③ Astrocyte foot processes — wrap around the outside; secrete growth factors that stimulate more tight junction formation in endothelial cells

What crosses the BBB?

✓ Freely crosses: O₂, CO₂, lipid-soluble molecules (some drugs, vitamins)
⇄ Requires transport: water, Na⁺, Cl⁻, glucose (GLUT1 on BBB; GLUT3 on neurons)
✗ Blocked: large proteins — would cause undesired neural stimulation or inhibition

Where the BBB is intentionally absent

Circumventricular organs
  • Area postrema (medulla) — samples blood for toxins, ketones, and drugs → triggers chemoreceptor trigger zone → vomiting reflex
  • Osmoreceptors near hypothalamus (subfornicular organ, OVLT) — sense blood osmolarity → regulate thirst, ADH release, and water balance
  • Hypothalamic-pituitary axis — releasing hormones (CRH, GHRH, GnRH) must travel from hypothalamus to anterior pituitary via the portal bloodstream
Key insight: Astrocytes don't just form a physical layer — they chemically maintain the barrier. Damage to astrocytes → fewer tight junctions → increased BBB permeability → undesired molecules reach neural tissue → dysregulated neural activity.

Function 2 — Potassium buffering

Why excess extracellular K⁺ is a problem

Spatial buffering
  • During APs and at rest (via leak channels), K⁺ exits neurons; Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase recycles some, but extracellular K⁺ can still accumulate
  • High extracellular K⁺ → reduced concentration gradient → less K⁺ leaves → more positive charge inside → initially increased excitability, then inactivation of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels → decreased excitability
Astrocyte solution: Take up excess K⁺ via channels. If one astrocyte is overloaded, it passes K⁺ through gap junctions to neighboring astrocytes ("spatial buffering"). Can also release K⁺ back if local levels are depleted.

Function 4 — Glucose & glycogen metabolism

Lactate shuttle to neurons

Energy reserve
  • Astrocytes take up glucose via GLUT1; neurons use GLUT3
  • Astrocytes can store glucose as glycogen (glycogenesis) — act as on-demand energy reserve
  • When neuron ATP drops: astrocyte breaks down glycogen → glucose → pyruvate → lactate
  • Lactate transported out of astrocyte → into neuron → converted back to pyruvate → enters Krebs cycle → ATP production

Function 5 — Synaptic modulation

Synaptic enhancement

  • Astrocytes can increase synapse formation and enhance synaptic function between neurons
  • Exact molecular mechanism is still an active area of research

Satellite cells

PNS only
  • The PNS equivalent of astrocytes — perform most of the same core functions
  • Perform: potassium buffering, neurotransmitter regulation, nutrient/glucose control
  • Do not form a blood-brain barrier equivalent (no comparable vascular wrapping role in the PNS)

Where satellite cells are found

Dorsal root ganglia

Sensory
  • Surround cell bodies of sensory neurons in the dorsal root
  • Provide structural and metabolic support to pseudo-unipolar neurons

Autonomic ganglia

Motor
  • Surround cell bodies of sympathetic neurons (paravertebral and prevertebral ganglia)
  • Surround cell bodies of parasympathetic neurons (terminal ganglia, near target organs)
Memory hook: Satellite cells = astrocytes of the PNS, but without the BBB role. Everywhere there's a ganglion (a cluster of neuronal cell bodies outside the CNS), satellite cells are present to support them.

Myelinating glial cells — comparison

Myelinating cells
Feature Oligodendrocytes Schwann cells

Schwann cell structure — how myelination works

Unmyelinated state

  • Schwann cell extends arms to partially wrap the axon
  • Incomplete coverage — axon membrane exposed along its length
  • Action potentials propagate continuously along every segment

Myelinated state

  • Schwann cell membrane wraps repeatedly in concentric layers around the axon
  • Myelin sheath — the concentric lipid-protein layers formed by wrapping
  • Neurolemma — the outermost Schwann cell membrane layer, outside the sheath; enables PNS regeneration after GBS damage

Nodes of Ranvier

Action potential sites
  • Small gaps between adjacent myelin segments along the axon
  • Each node is densely packed with voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels
  • These gaps are the only sites where action potentials can be regenerated in myelinated axons
  • The basis of saltatory conduction — potentials "jump" from node to node
Rx
Multiple sclerosis (MS)

Autoimmune demyelination of oligodendrocytes in the CNS. No regeneration possible → progressive conduction deficits. CNS axons lose insulation permanently.

Rx
Guillain-Barré (GBS)

Autoimmune demyelination of Schwann cells in the PNS. Recovery is possible because the neurolemma survives and guides axon regrowth.

What myelin is and does

Insulator
  • Myelin = lipid + protein — a multi-layered membrane wrap around the axon
  • Acts as an electrical insulator, dramatically increasing action potential conduction velocity
  • Concentrates ion channels at nodes of Ranvier rather than along the entire axon

Two types of conduction

Saltatory conduction

Myelinated
  • AP "jumps" from one node of Ranvier to the next
  • Voltage-gated channels absent under the myelin — ions must travel down the axon to reach the next node
  • Faster — fewer regeneration events required

Continuous conduction

Unmyelinated
  • AP propagates step-by-step along the entire membrane
  • Ion channels distributed continuously — every patch of membrane regenerates the AP
  • Slower — every segment must depolarize individually

Why myelin speeds conduction — two mechanisms

No ion channels under the sheath

Mechanism 1
  • Voltage-gated Na⁺/K⁺ channels exist only at nodes
  • Ions cannot leak across myelinated regions
  • Ionic current flows efficiently down the axon to the next node without dissipating

Decreased membrane capacitance

Mechanism 2
  • Myelin reduces negative charge on the axon membrane
  • Fewer negative charges means fewer positive ions are "captured" as they flow forward
  • More ions travel onward → greater velocity

Ependymal cells

CNS only
  • Cuboidal/columnar cells that line the ventricles of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord
  • Key component of the blood-CSF barrier
  • Have cilia on their apical surface to propel CSF through the ventricular system

Blood-CSF barrier vs Blood-brain barrier

Blood-CSF barrier

① Endothelial cells — fenestrated (leaky) here; no tight junctions between them
② Basal lamina — connective tissue, same as BBB
③ Ependymal cells — connected by tight junctions. This is the selective barrier layer

Blood-brain barrier (recap)

① Endothelial cells — tight junctions here (the main barrier)
② Basal lamina
③ Astrocyte foot processes — chemically maintain, no tight junctions
Key distinction: In the BBB, tight junctions are between endothelial cells. In the blood-CSF barrier, tight junctions are between ependymal cells — the endothelial layer is leaky.

Three core functions

⇄

Microglia

CNS only
  • The immune cells of the CNS
  • Originate from monocytes produced in the red bone marrow
  • Monocytes migrate to the CNS and differentiate into microglia — unique lineage from all other glia

Activation trigger

What activates microglia

Danger signals
  • Neural damage or infection → release of cellular debris and cytokines
  • Microglia detect these signals via pattern-recognition receptors → become activated
  • Transition from a ramified (surveying) state to an amoeboid (active) morphology

Functions of activated microglia

Inflammatory response

Defense
  • Release nitric oxide, reactive oxygen species (free radicals), and pro-inflammatory cytokines
  • Goal: destroy pathogens causing neural damage
  • Side effect: can damage surrounding neural tissue and myelin → bystander demyelination

Phagocytosis & antigen presentation

Adaptive immunity
  • Phagocytose pathogens and cellular debris to clear the injury site
  • Present pathogen fragments on MHC II molecules on their surface
  • T cells cross the BBB → interact with MHC II → become activated → release cytokines → amplify and direct the immune response
Rx
Clinical pearl — HIV encephalitis

HIV preferentially attacks microglia in the CNS. This causes hyperactivation → excessive release of nitric oxide and free radicals → widespread demyelination of axons → HIV encephalitis.

This is why CNS deterioration (cognitive decline, motor deficits) can occur in advanced HIV infection even before opportunistic infections take hold.
