Section 01
Boundaries & Sulci
Three defining anatomical borders
Anterior boundary
Central sulcus — separates the parietal lobe from the frontal lobe. Immediately posterior to it lies the post-central gyrus (primary somatosensory cortex).
Inferior boundary
Lateral sulcus (Sylvian fissure) — separates the parietal lobe from the temporal lobe below.
Posterior boundary
Parieto-occipital sulcus — separates the parietal lobe from the occipital lobe posteriorly.
Section 02
Functional Areas Overview
Three major regions, each with a distinct sensory role

① Primary Somatosensory Cortex

Areas 3, 1, 2
  • Located just posterior to the central sulcus on the post-central gyrus
  • Function: conscious awareness of somatic sensations — touch, pain, temperature, vibration, pressure, proprioception
  • Brodmann areas 3, 1, 2 (often remembered as 1-2-3 in posterior order, but 3 is the primary input zone)

② Somatosensory Association Cortex

Higher-order
  • Located posterior to the primary somatosensory cortex
  • Function: analyzes, recognizes, and stores somatic sensations in memory
  • Gives meaning to what we feel — object identity, texture, shape, weight

③ Posterior Association Area

Multi-modal
  • Spans parts of the parietal, occipital, and temporal lobes (parieto-occipito-temporal cortex)
  • Function: multi-modal integration — combines visual, auditory, and somatic sensations
  • Essential for spatial coordination and making coherent sense of the environment
Section 03
Primary Somatosensory Cortex
Post-central gyrus · Brodmann Areas 3, 1, 2

Two ascending input pathways

Dorsal column–medial lemniscus (DCML)

Discriminative
Fine / discriminative touch Proprioception Vibration Kinesthesia
Route: Peripheral receptor → dorsal column → nucleus gracilis/cuneatus (medulla) → decussates → medial lemniscus → thalamus (VPL) → primary somatosensory cortex

Spinothalamic tract (STT)

Protective
Pain Temperature Crude touch Pressure
Route: Peripheral receptor → dorsal horn (spinal cord) → decussates immediately → anterolateral column → thalamus (VPL) → primary somatosensory cortex
!
Key rule — contralateral processing

Both the DCML and STT decussate (cross) before reaching the cortex. Sensations from the right body → left primary somatosensory cortex, and vice versa. They differ only in where they cross: DCML crosses in the medulla; STT crosses at the spinal cord level of entry.

Motor contribution — an often-overlooked fact

~40% of CST
  • The primary somatosensory cortex is not purely sensory — it contributes approximately 40% of the fibers in the corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts
  • This makes it a significant participant in motor function alongside the frontal motor areas
  • Damage here causes both sensory deficits and motor weakness
Section 04
Somatotopic Arrangement & Sensory Homunculus
Body map on the post-central gyrus

The primary somatosensory cortex is organized somatotopically — different body regions map to distinct cortical zones. This map is called the sensory homunculus. Arrangement mirrors the motor homunculus (Unit 4): medial cortex = lower body; lateral cortex = upper body and head.

Hip
Trunk
Shoulder
Arm
Hand

Tongue
← Medial (superior) — ACA territory MCA territory — Lateral (inferior) →
Medial cortex → ACA territory
Receives sensations from lower limbs (foot → lower leg → thigh → trunk). ACA occlusion → contralateral lower extremity sensory loss.
Lateral cortex → MCA territory
Receives sensations from upper limbs and head/neck (arm → hand → face → tongue). MCA occlusion → contralateral upper extremity + face/neck sensory loss.
Homunculus distortion rule: Body parts like hands and face appear disproportionately large. Larger cortical representation = greater sensory nerve density = higher sensitivity in that region. Fingertips and lips have far more mechanoreceptors per cm² than the back or thigh.
Rx
Clinical correlation — Vascular lesions & sensory loss

ACA occlusion → medial somatosensory cortex → contralateral lower extremity sensory loss (foot, leg). Often accompanied by lower extremity motor weakness (motor homunculus also medial).

MCA occlusion → lateral somatosensory cortex → contralateral upper extremity + head & neck sensory loss. Often co-occurs with Broca's aphasia and upper extremity weakness.

Section 05
Somatosensory Association Cortex
Analyze · recognize · store somatic sensations

Processing hierarchy

① Peripheral receptors — mechanoreceptors, nociceptors, thermoreceptors in skin, muscle, joints
② DCML / STT → thalamus (VPL nucleus) → primary somatosensory cortex — conscious perception is generated here
③ Somatosensory association cortex — analyze · recognize · store. Gives the sensation meaning.

Three higher-order functions

Analysis
Evaluates texture, weight, size, edges, and position in 3D space — the fine details of an object's physical properties.
Pattern recognition
Compares incoming sensations against stored memories to identify objects (e.g., feeling a key vs. a coin with eyes closed).
Memory storage
Stores sensory experiences for future reference — builds the library of tactile memories used for recognition.
Section 06
Clinical Exam Findings
Neuro exam tests for somatosensory association cortex damage

Damage to the somatosensory association cortex produces characteristic deficits testable on the neurological exam. These tests all require eyes closed so the patient must rely purely on tactile information.

Test Method Clinical term Deficit meaning
Object ID Place an object (eraser, coin, key) in the patient's hand; eyes closed; ask them to identify it. Stereognosis Inability = astereognosis Cannot identify objects by touch alone — failure of shape/texture pattern recognition.
Symbol tracing Draw a number or letter on the patient's palm with a fingertip; patient identifies it. Graphesthesia Inability = agraphesthesia Cannot identify symbols traced on skin — loss of fine spatial mapping of touch.
Finger position Move patient's finger up or down with their eyes closed; patient states the direction. Statognosis Inability = astatognosis Cannot identify body part position — impaired proprioceptive processing.
Weight discrimination Place different weights sequentially in the patient's hand; patient identifies which is heavier. Barognosis Inability = baragnosis Cannot distinguish weight differences — failure of pressure magnitude comparison.
Important distinction: These deficits occur with association cortex damage, not primary cortex damage. If the primary cortex is damaged, the patient loses all sensation in that area. If only the association cortex is damaged, basic sensation is preserved but the patient cannot interpret what they feel.
Section 07
Posterior Association Area
Parieto-occipito-temporal cortex · multi-modal integration

A multi-modal association area that bridges the parietal, occipital, and temporal lobes. Its core job is to combine all three major sensory streams into a single coherent perception of the world.

Three sensory inputs

Visual
👁
Visual association cortex → (occipital lobe)
Auditory
👂
Auditory association cortex → (temporal lobe)
Somatic
✋
Somatosensory association cortex → (parietal lobe)
all three converge
Posterior Association Area
Unified spatial perception of the world

Two outputs

→ Prefrontal cortex
Elaboration of thought, executive decision-making, and encoding of multi-sensory events into long-term memory.
→ Motor / pre-motor cortex
Coordinates movement responses based on the integrated sensory picture — turns perception into action.

Worked example — HCl beaker scenario

Dropping a beaker of hydrochloric acid

Multi-modal
① Somatic signal — acid contact on foot → nociceptors → STT → somatosensory cortex → posterior association area
② Auditory signal — crash sound → cochlea → auditory cortex (temporal) → posterior association area
③ Visual signal — seeing it happen → retina → visual cortex (occipital) → posterior association area
④ All three converge → prefrontal cortex (decision-making, memory formation) → motor cortex (escape response)
Primary function summary: Spatial coordination and the integration of multi-modal sensory experience into coherent perception — the neurological basis for making sense of the environment using all senses simultaneously.
