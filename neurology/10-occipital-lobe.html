Section 01
Boundaries of the Occipital Lobe
One real sulcus · one imaginary line
Superior boundary — real sulcus
Parieto-occipital sulcus — a clearly defined sulcus on the medial surface. Separates the occipital lobe from the parietal lobe above and anterior to it.
Anterior boundary — imaginary line
Preoccipital notch → tip of lateral sulcus — an imaginary line connecting these two landmarks separates the occipital lobe from the temporal lobe anteriorly.
Key distinction: The parieto-occipital sulcus is a real, identifiable anatomical structure. The anterior boundary is imaginary — drawn by convention between two landmarks to define where temporal ends and occipital begins.
Section 02
Two Cortical Areas
Primary visual cortex · visual association cortex

The occipital lobe contains exactly two functionally distinct cortices — a primary sensory area and a higher-order association area. They work in strict sequence: primary cortex first, association cortex second.

① Primary Visual Cortex

Location: Most posterior part of the occipital lobe, along the calcarine sulcus (medial surface)

Brodmann Area: 17

Core function: Conscious awareness of visual stimuli — knowing that a visual object is present

Receives the raw image but does not yet identify or interpret it.

② Visual Association Cortex

Location: Anterior to the primary visual cortex, surrounding it

Brodmann Areas: 18, 19

Core function: Meaning, understanding, and recognition of visual stimuli

Analyzes the raw image, compares it with stored memories, and names it.

!
Two-stage analogy

Think of it as a two-stage process: the primary visual cortex says "something is there" — the visual association cortex says "I know exactly what that is."

Section 03
Visual Pathway
From external object to conscious recognition — step by step

Complete visual pathway

①
Object — light reflected from the object enters the eye through the cornea and lens
②
Retina — photoreceptors (rods for low light, cones for color/acuity) transduce light energy into neural signals via retinal ganglion cells
③
Optic nerve (CN II) — carries signals from each eye toward the optic chiasma
④
Optic chiasma — partial decussation: nasal fibers cross to the contralateral optic tract; temporal fibers remain ipsilateral. Each optic tract carries signals from the contralateral visual field of both eyes
⑤
Lateral Geniculate Nucleus (LGN) of the thalamus — main visual relay station; preserves retinotopic organization
⑥
Optic radiations (geniculocalcarine tract) — fan of white matter fibers projecting from LGN to the calcarine sulcus
⑦
Primary visual cortex (V1 · Area 17) — calcarine sulcus, medial occipital lobe → conscious awareness of the image
⑧
Visual association cortex (Areas 18, 19) — analysis, memory comparison, and recognition of the object

Decussation at the optic chiasma — what crosses?

Nasal retinal fibers — cross
Carry signals from the temporal (outer) visual field. Cross at the chiasma to the contralateral optic tract.
Temporal retinal fibers — stay
Carry signals from the nasal (inner) visual field. Remain ipsilateral — do not cross.
Net result: Each primary visual cortex receives input from the opposite visual field of both eyes. The right primary visual cortex processes the left visual field as seen by both the left and right eyes — not just the left eye.
Rx
Pathway lesion correlations

Optic nerve cut → monocular blindness in that eye (all signals from one eye lost).

Optic chiasma lesion (e.g., pituitary adenoma pressing from below) → bitemporal hemianopia — loss of both temporal visual fields (the crossing nasal fibers are cut).

Optic tract / LGN / optic radiation / V1 lesion → contralateral homonymous hemianopia — loss of the same half of the visual field in both eyes.

Section 04
Visual Association Cortex — Detail
Areas 18 & 19 · analysis · recognition · two output streams

What it analyzes — four properties

🎨
Color
Wavelength discrimination — hue, saturation, brightness
📐
Size & shape
Object dimensions, angles, outlines, contours
🔄
Motion
Moving vs. stationary; speed and direction of motion
📍
Location / depth
Spatial position, distance, binocular disparity

Two-step recognition process

①Analyze — break down the visual properties (color, size, shape, motion) of the incoming image from V1
② Compare with past memories — match the analyzed properties against stored visual memory traces → Recognition

Two output streams

Ventral stream — "What"
Projects to inferior temporal cortex. Handles object identity — what an object is (color, shape, face recognition). Damage → visual agnosia, prosopagnosia.
Dorsal stream — "Where/How"
Projects to posterior parietal cortex. Handles spatial location and visual guidance of action — where an object is and how to reach it. Damage → visual-spatial neglect, optic ataxia.
Basketball example: Your eyes deliver raw visual data to V1 (primary cortex). The association cortex then extracts: orange color, spherical shape, black seam lines, bouncing motion — compares all four against stored memory — and concludes: "basketball." Without this step you could see it but not name it.
Section 05
Visual Agnosia
Clinical correlation — sees but cannot recognize

Definition

Association cortex lesion
  • The inability to recognize a visual object despite having intact vision
  • Lesion site: visual association cortex (Areas 18/19) — not the primary visual cortex
  • The entire visual pathway up to and including V1 is functioning normally
  • The failure is in interpretation of the image, not its reception

What is intact vs. impaired

Structure Status Implication
Retina (photoreceptors) ✓ Intact Light transduced normally into neural signals
Optic nerve (CN II) ✓ Intact Signal travels normally toward the brain
Optic chiasma & tracts ✓ Intact Decussation and relay proceed normally
LGN (thalamus) ✓ Intact Visual relay station functioning normally
Primary visual cortex (V1) ✓ Intact Patient can see — conscious awareness preserved
Visual association cortex ✗ Lesioned Patient cannot recognize — analysis and memory comparison fail
!
Clinical scenario

The patient is shown a basketball. They can describe seeing "an orange, round object" (V1 is working — conscious awareness intact). But they cannot identify it as a basketball — the association cortex lesion has severed the link between visual analysis and stored object memory.

They see it perfectly. They simply cannot recognize it.

Contrast with cortical blindness: A primary visual cortex lesion causes cortical blindness — the patient has no conscious visual awareness at all (cannot see). A visual association cortex lesion causes visual agnosia — vision is present but recognition is lost.
