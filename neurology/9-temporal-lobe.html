Section 01
Boundaries of the Temporal Lobe
Two anatomical borders
Superior boundary
Lateral sulcus (Sylvian fissure) — clearly visible on the lateral surface. Separates the temporal lobe from both the frontal lobe and the parietal lobe above it.
Posterior boundary
Preoccipital notch (imaginary line) — a small notch at the posterior end of the brain. An imaginary line drawn from here toward the tip of the lateral sulcus separates the temporal lobe from the occipital lobe.
Section 02
Primary Auditory Cortex
Superior temporal gyrus · Brodmann Areas 41 & 42

Location & core function

Conscious sound
  • Located on the superior temporal gyrus, lateral surface of the temporal lobe
  • Responsible for conscious awareness of sound — specifically frequency, pitch, and localization

Three properties perceived

〜
Frequency
Rate of sound wave oscillations (Hz)
♪
Pitch
Perceived highness or lowness of a tone
◎
Localization
Spatial origin of a sound (left vs. right)

Auditory pathway to cortex

①Cochlea (Spiral Organ of Corti) — hair cells transduce sound waves into neural signals
②CN VIII (vestibulocochlear nerve) — carries signals from cochlea to brainstem
③Cochlear nuclei (medulla) — first central synapse; signal partially decussates via the trapezoid body
④Medial geniculate nucleus (thalamus) — auditory relay; tonotopic organization preserved
⑤ Primary auditory cortex — contralateral dominance; conscious sound perception begins here
!
Key rule — contralateral dominance

Auditory signals are predominantly contralateral: sound from the left ear → right primary auditory cortex (crosses mainly at cochlear nuclei). Unlike vision or somatic sensation, auditory input is not exclusively contralateral — there is some bilateral representation — so a unilateral cortical lesion does not cause complete deafness.

Rx
Lesion of primary auditory cortex

Damage above the cochlear nuclei (e.g., right primary auditory cortex) results in:

  • Difficulty localizing sound from the contralateral (left) side
  • Impaired ability to distinguish pitch and frequency of contralateral sounds
  • Not complete deafness — bilateral representation provides some redundancy
Section 03
Auditory Association Cortex
Inferior to primary auditory cortex · gives meaning to sounds

Location & function

Meaning
  • Located inferior to the primary auditory cortex on the temporal lobe
  • Receives processed input from the primary auditory cortex
  • Gives meaning and purpose to sounds — transforms acoustic data into recognized patterns

Four-step processing sequence

① Receive
Gets pitch, frequency, amplitude, and localization data from the primary auditory cortex
② Analyze
Processes and dissects all acoustic properties of the incoming sound
③ Compare
Matches the sound against past memories of previously heard sounds stored in the temporal lobe
④ Interpret
Generates a recognized meaning or purpose — e.g., distinguishing tone of voice: "I'm fine" (content) vs. "I'm fine" (angry)
Distinction from Wernicke's: The auditory association cortex gives meaning to all sounds (music, environmental noise, speech). Wernicke's area (below) specifically handles language comprehension — it builds on auditory association processing but adds linguistic interpretation.
Section 04
Wernicke's Area
Posterior superior temporal gyrus · Brodmann Area 22 · language comprehension

Location & core function

Comprehension
  • Located in the posterior temporal lobe, inferior to the primary auditory cortex
  • Dominant hemisphere (usually left in right-handed individuals)
  • Responsible for comprehension and understanding of both spoken and written language

Two inputs to Wernicke's area

Auditory input
Processed sound (via primary + auditory association cortex) → meaning of spoken words
Visual input
Facial expressions & written text (via visual cortices) → non-verbal and written language cues

Output pathway — Wernicke's → Broca's

Wernicke's Area → Arcuate Fasciculus → Broca's Area (Frontal) → Corticobulbar tract → Speech production (CN VII / IX / X / XII)

The arcuate fasciculus is the white matter bundle physically connecting Wernicke's to Broca's area.

Rx
Wernicke's Aphasia (Receptive Aphasia)

Cause: damage to Wernicke's area — most commonly from a middle cerebral artery (MCA) stroke to the dominant hemisphere.

  • Fluent speech — Broca's area is intact, so speech production is preserved
  • Nonsensical content — speech is "word salad," grammatically possible but meaningless
  • Impaired comprehension — cannot understand what others are saying (spoken or written)
Broca's aphasia (expressive)
Area: Frontal lobe (Areas 44/45)
Speech: non-fluent, effortful
Comprehension: intact
"Understands but can't speak"
Wernicke's aphasia (receptive)
Area: Temporal lobe (Area 22)
Speech: fluent but word salad
Comprehension: impaired
"Speaks but can't understand"
!
Memory tip

Wernicke = Comprehension. Damage → can speak fluently but output is meaningless and cannot understand others.
Broca = Production. Damage → understands everything but speech is laboured and halting.

Section 05
Primary Olfactory & Association Cortex
Uncus · medial temporal lobe · smell and emotional memory

Location

Medial surface
  • Found on the medial surface of the temporal lobe — not visible from the lateral surface
  • Located at the uncus (hook-shaped anterior medial temporal structure)
  • A paired olfactory association cortex lies just beneath the primary cortex

Olfactory pathway — from nose to cortex

①Odorant molecules enter the nasal cavity and bind olfactory receptor proteins
②Olfactory receptors (nasal epithelium) — bipolar neurons; the only neurons that regenerate throughout life
③CN I (olfactory nerve fibers) pass through the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone
④Olfactory bulb — first synapse; mitral cells project posteriorly
⑤Olfactory tract → splits into lateral olfactory striae (main pathway) and medial olfactory striae (to orbital frontal cortex)
⑥ Lateral olfactory striae → Primary olfactory cortex at the uncus

Primary vs association cortex — different roles

Primary olfactory cortex
Conscious awareness of smell — knowing that something is present to smell. Simple detection, not identification.
Olfactory association cortex
Analyzes odorant composition · Compares with stored smell memories · Identifies the smell (loss = anosmia) · Links to amygdala → emotional associations
Why smells trigger vivid emotional memories: The olfactory association cortex projects directly to the amygdala (emotional processing center) — a more direct route than any other sensory modality, which all pass through the thalamus first. This is why a specific scent can instantly and powerfully evoke a distant emotional memory.
Section 06
Insula (Insular Cortex)
Deep in lateral sulcus · the 5th lobe · taste, visceral, vestibular

Location & classification

5th lobe
  • Buried deep within the lateral sulcus, hidden between temporal and parietal lobes
  • Technically considered a fifth lobe of the cerebral cortex — not part of the temporal lobe proper
  • Only visible when the lateral sulcus is opened or in coronal section

Three functional roles

① Taste (Gustation)

Primary gustatory cortex
  • Receives taste signals from CN VII (chorda tympani — anterior 2/3 of tongue), CN IX (posterior 1/3), and CN X (epiglottis/pharynx)
  • All three converge on the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) in the medulla → thalamus → insula
  • Allows conscious discrimination of the five tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami

② Visceral sensations

Interoception
  • Receives pain and temperature signals from heart, lungs, and GI tract
  • Contributes to visceral memory — e.g., remembering food poisoning to avoid that food in the future
  • Involved in the subjective "gut feeling" component of decision-making

③ Vestibular sensations

Proposed cortical site
  • Proposed as the vestibular cortex — site of cortical equilibrium awareness
  • Receives input from semicircular canals (dynamic equilibrium: head rotation) and vestibule (static equilibrium: head tilt, linear acceleration)
  • Less established than taste and visceral functions — still under active investigation
