Section 01
Overview of the Cerebral Cortex
Five lobes · universal primary → association → integration pattern

The cerebral cortex is divided into four main lobes — frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital — plus a fifth, smaller lobe called the insula, which is hidden within the lateral sulcus and only visible when that sulcus is opened.

Each lobe follows a universal two-tier architecture: a primary cortex that receives or sends raw signals, paired with an association cortex that analyses, interprets, and stores those signals. Higher-order integration occurs in the posterior association area, where parietal, occipital, and temporal signals converge.

Universal cortical processing pattern

Primary Cortex Raw signal — awareness only
→
Association Cortex Analyse · recognise · store
→
Posterior Association Area Multi-modal integration
→
Motor Output / Memory Action · long-term encoding

Five lobes at a glance

Frontal lobe
Motor control, executive function, speech production. Anterior to central sulcus; superior to lateral sulcus.
Temporal lobe
Auditory processing, language comprehension, olfaction. Inferior to lateral sulcus.
Parietal lobe
Somatosensory processing, spatial integration. Posterior to central sulcus; anterior to parieto-occipital sulcus.
Occipital lobe
Visual processing and recognition. Posterior to parieto-occipital sulcus and imaginary preoccipital line.
Insula (5th lobe)
Taste, visceral sensation, vestibular processing. Hidden within the lateral sulcus — only visible when the sulcus is retracted.
Section 02
Sulci & Lobar Demarcations
Four boundaries that define all lobe territories
Central Sulcus
Separates Frontal (anterior) from Parietal (posterior). Pre-central gyrus = motor cortex; post-central gyrus = sensory cortex.
Lateral Sulcus
aka Sylvian fissure
Separates Temporal (inferior) from Frontal & Parietal (superior). Insula is hidden within.
Parieto-occipital Sulcus
Real sulcus on medial surface
Separates Parietal (anterior) from Occipital (posterior).
Preoccipital Notch → Lateral Sulcus tip
Imaginary convention line
Separates Occipital (posterior) from Temporal (anterior).
Section 03
Frontal Lobe
Anterior to central sulcus · superior to lateral sulcus · 5 functional areas
Frontal Lobe — Functional Areas
Brodmann Areas 4, 6, 8, 9–47, 44/45
AreaPositionFunction
Primary Motor Cortex
Contralateral control
BA 4
Pre-central gyrus
Immediately anterior to central sulcus
Voluntary movement of the contralateral body. Left PMC controls right body; right PMC controls left. Outputs via corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts.
Motor Association Cortex
BA 6 (pre-motor + SMA)
Anterior to PMC
Pre-motor (lateral) + Supplementary Motor Area (medial)
Planning, sequencing, and execution of voluntary movement. Contributes ~15% of corticospinal fibers; targets axial and proximal musculature.
Frontal Eye Fields
BA 8
Anterior & superior to motor assoc. cortex
Voluntary conjugate saccadic eye movements. Each FEF drives gaze to the contralateral side via the PPRF → CN VI nucleus → MLF → CN III.
Prefrontal Cortex
BA 9–14, 24, 25, 32, 45–47
Anterior to FEF
Prefrontal association area
Memory & learning · Personality & behaviour · Judgment, reasoning, decision-making · Working memory. Projects to motor association cortex for planning.
Broca's Area
Dominant hemisphere
BA 44/45
Inferior frontal gyrus (usually left)
Speech production — generates the motor program for spoken language; outputs via corticobulbar tract to CN VII, IX/X/XI, XII.
💡
Dominant hemisphere: Broca's area is found on the dominant cerebral hemisphere (left in ~95% of right-handed individuals). Damage → expressive (non-fluent) aphasia — understands but cannot produce fluent speech.
Section 04
Temporal Lobe
Inferior to lateral sulcus · auditory · language · olfaction
Temporal Lobe — Functional Areas
Brodmann Areas 41, 42, 22, + medial olfactory cortex
AreaPositionFunction
Primary Auditory Cortex
BA 41, 42
Superior temporal gyrus
Just below lateral sulcus
Conscious awareness of sound pitch, frequency, and spatial location. Receives from CN VIII → cochlear nuclei → medial geniculate nucleus (thalamus). Contralateral dominance.
Auditory Association Cortex
Inferior to primary auditory cortex
Analyses pitch, frequency, amplitude, tone · Compares with stored auditory memories · Identifies sounds (musical notes, speech phonemes, alarms).
Wernicke's Area
Dominant hemisphere
BA 22
Posterior superior temporal gyrus
Near temporal–parietal junction
Comprehension and understanding of spoken and written language. Projects to Broca's via arcuate fasciculus. Lesion → receptive (fluent) aphasia — word salad speech, impaired comprehension.
Primary Olfactory Cortex
+ Olfactory Association Cortex
Medial temporal lobe
Uncus — not visible on lateral surface
Primary: awareness of smell (CN I → cribriform plate → olfactory bulb → lateral striae → uncus). Association: identifies & stores smells; projects to amygdala for emotional scent-memory.
💡
Wernicke's vs Broca's: Wernicke's = language comprehension (temporal lobe). Broca's = language production (frontal lobe). Both dominant hemisphere. Wernicke's aphasia is fluent but meaningless; Broca's is non-fluent but understood.
Section 05
Parietal Lobe
Posterior to central sulcus · somatosensory · spatial integration
Parietal Lobe — Functional Areas
Brodmann Areas 3/1/2, 5/7, 39/40
AreaPositionFunction
Primary Somatosensory Cortex
BA 3, 1, 2
Post-central gyrus
Immediately posterior to central sulcus
Conscious awareness of somatic sensations: fine/discriminative touch (DCML pathway), proprioception, vibration, pressure, pain, and temperature (STT pathway). Somatotopic organisation = sensory homunculus.
Somatosensory Association Cortex
BA 5, 7
Posterior to primary somatosensory cortex
Analyses & recognises somatic sensations — stereognosis (object ID by touch), graphesthesia (symbol on skin), barognosis (weight discrimination), statognosis (joint position).
Posterior Association Area
BA 39, 40
Posterior parietal cortex
Angular & supramarginal gyri
Multi-modal integration of visual, auditory, and somatosensory association cortices. Generates unified spatial awareness of the environment. Outputs to prefrontal cortex and motor areas.
💡
Multi-modal convergence: The posterior association area is where vision + hearing + touch all meet — the neurological foundation for coherent perception of where you are, what surrounds you, and how to navigate it.
Section 06
Occipital Lobe
Posterior to parieto-occipital sulcus · visual awareness & recognition
Occipital Lobe — Functional Areas
Brodmann Areas 17, 18, 19
AreaPositionFunction
Primary Visual Cortex (V1)
BA 17
Calcarine sulcus
Most posterior occipital (medial)
Conscious awareness of visual stimuli. Receives from retina → CN II → optic chiasma → LGN (thalamus) → optic radiations → calcarine cortex. Contralateral visual field representation.
Visual Association Cortex
BA 18, 19
Surrounding and anterior to V1
Analyses color, size, shape, and motion · Compares with stored visual memories · Recognises objects. Outputs: ventral ("what") stream → temporal; dorsal ("where/how") stream → parietal.
💡
V1 says "something is there." The visual association cortex says "I know what that is." A V1 lesion → cortical blindness. An association cortex lesion → visual agnosia (sees but cannot recognise).
Section 07
Insula — The 5th Lobe
Hidden in the lateral sulcus · taste · visceral · vestibular
Insula — Functional Areas
Visible only when the lateral sulcus is retracted · BA 13
FunctionInputsDetail
Gustation (Taste)
Primary gustatory cortex
CN VII (anterior ⅔ tongue), CN IX (posterior ⅓), CN X (epiglottis) → nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS, medulla) → thalamus → insula
Conscious awareness and discrimination of the five tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami. Association cortex layers identify specific foods.
Visceral Sensation
Interoception
GVA fibers from heart, lungs, GI tract → brainstem → thalamus → insula
Awareness of internal organ sensations — gut pain, nausea, cardiac sensation. Contributes to visceral memory (e.g., food aversion after poisoning).
Vestibular Cortex
Proposed site
Semicircular canals (dynamic equilibrium) + vestibule (static equilibrium) → CN VIII → vestibular nuclei → thalamus → insula
Proposed cortical site for equilibrium processing — dynamic (rotational acceleration) and static (linear head tilt). Still under active investigation.

Section 09
Clinical Correlates
Eight lesion syndromes — one card per condition
Frontal — Broca's Area

Broca's Aphasia

Damage to Broca's area (dominant inferior frontal gyrus, usually MCA territory) → non-fluent (expressive) aphasia. Patient understands language but cannot produce fluent, grammatical speech. Often co-presents with right-sided upper extremity weakness.

Temporal — Wernicke's Area

Wernicke's Aphasia

Damage to Wernicke's area (dominant posterior temporal, MCA) → fluent aphasia with impaired comprehension. Speech is effortless but meaningless ("word salad"). Patient cannot understand spoken or written language.

Parietal — Somatosensory Assoc.

Astereognosis

Lesion of somatosensory association cortex → inability to identify objects by touch alone despite intact primary somatosensory sensation. Also associated: agraphesthesia (can't identify symbols traced on skin), baragnosis (can't compare weights).

Parietal — Posterior Association Area

Hemispatial Neglect

Usually right posterior association area (non-dominant parietal) lesion → patient ignores the contralateral (left) half of space — inattention, failure to dress left side, omitting left side of drawings. Sensory pathways intact; interpretation is disrupted.

Frontal — Prefrontal Cortex

Prefrontal Syndrome

Damage to prefrontal cortex (e.g., Phineas Gage; frontotemporal dementia) → personality change, poor impulse control, impaired judgment, disinhibition, hypersexuality, working memory loss, and late parkinsonian motor features.

Occipital Lobe

Cortical Blindness / Visual Agnosia

Primary visual cortex (V1) lesion → cortical blindness — patient has no conscious visual awareness despite intact eyes. Visual association cortex lesion → visual agnosia — patient can see but cannot recognise or name what they see.

Frontal — Frontal Eye Fields

Frontal Eye Field Lesion

Destructive (stroke) lesion of FEF → eyes deviate toward the lesion side (ipsilateral) — unopposed contralateral FEF drives gaze away from the lesion. Irritative (seizure) lesion → eyes deviate away from the focus (contralateral).

Temporal — Olfactory Cortex

Anosmia / Olfactory Agnosia

Primary olfactory cortex (uncus) damage → anosmia — loss of conscious smell awareness. Association cortex damage → olfactory agnosia — can detect smell but cannot identify or name the odour. Also: uncal herniation may compress CN III before olfactory symptoms appear.
